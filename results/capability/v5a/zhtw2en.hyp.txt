Please restore oversight of the state prison system because it is in people’s interests. The Washington prison system is facing an existential crisis. As the chair of our legislative working group that studies the state prison system and makes recommendations for change to help make prisons safer, we have been faced with one of these crises: an unsettling but unavoidable fact that despite our different backgrounds including prison management, civil rights advocacy, and some who’ve experienced incarceration, we all agree that Washington’s prisons must be overseen by independent oversight bodies.
Over 130,000 people were sent into the state prison system in 2022, yet there has been surprisingly little information about their post-incarceration status. There is no statewide office overseeing prisons and reporting on data to the public. Only part of this information relates to deaths during incarceration.
Our findings demonstrate that immediate action is required. Even if you include population growth in your calculations, the death rate in Washington prisons between 2000 and 2019 increased nearly threefold, which is 16 times higher than the national average. Washington now ranks fourth highest in the nation when it comes to incarceration-related mortality rates. This cannot continue.
To reverse this trend and address many other problems facing our prisons, difficult policy decisions may be necessary. But independent oversight of prisons is critical first step toward making prisons safer and more humane. Proven evidence shows that independent oversight systems increase transparency and accountability both in Washington and nationally. According to our statistics, at least 25 states have statewide offices overseeing prisons, and they all report lower incarceration-related mortality rates than Washington.
What’s interesting is that recently Washington has established or expanded statewide offices of oversight for state prisons, police, schools and other entities. Prisons are a notable exception when it comes to oversight of health and safety for millions of people.
Independent prison oversight offices can monitor prison conditions, interview staff and inmates, report on firsthand accounts, even express concerns before injuries occur. They can also collect and report data to assist administrators, policymakers and citizens understand our prison system and advocate for data-driven solutions to prison-related issues like incarceration-related mortality.
Oversight isn’t just good for incarcerated people. Our working group determined that prison oversight offices could collaborate with heavy-handed prison systems to provide urgent assistance and advice, such as smart policies that improve employee safety and reduce recidivism.
Washington briefly had a statewide prison oversight office from 1981 to 1987, and the Department of Justice once touted it as a model for the federal system. Sadly, despite opposition from prison managers, civil society groups and others, legislatures removed prison oversight institutions.
In areas where long-standing lack of transparency benefits communities, independent oversight will promote trust and data-driven decision-making. We ask Congress to enact the workgroup’s recommendation that restores the independence of city, county and regional prisons. It matters.
Haley O’Neill-Kagan actively campaigns against incarceration and serves as executive director of the community organization Beyond These Walls.

How to know if you're flying a Boeing 737 Max?
For most passengers, the type of aircraft is not a deciding factor when booking flights. But after a body part fell off on January 6th at Alaska Airlines, most Boeing 737 Max 9s were banned from operating in the United States, so some potential travelers may want to know what kind of plane they’re getting and which types are safest. The first question is easy to answer, but the second one is more complicated.
The type of aircraft usually appears in flight information for a flight. On Google Flights, this information is included in the details of each flight, and it’s often listed on each airline’s reservation page. If the type isn’t clearly marked on either Google Flights or an airline’s reservation page, sites like Expert Flyer and Seat Guru also aggregate flight information such as seat maps and aircraft types.
Determining which type of aircraft is safest can be more complicated. The National Transportation Safety Agency maintains a comprehensive database of accidents and incidents. The Federal Aviation Administration also provides preliminary accident reports on its “Airplane Accident Reporting and Sharing” page that include related manufacturers over the past ten days.
Another useful FAA link is a global summary of commercial jet accidents between 1959 and 2022, broken down by aircraft type, allowing airlines to assess the safety of their types. According to the report published in September 2023, the Boeing 787 and Airbus A350 are the least common types of aircraft that have been completely destroyed (the extent of damage beyond repair). Other types that never experienced a complete destruction include the twin-engine A380. However, these types had fewer than 1 million takeoffs during the reporting period.
The report also includes data on how frequently accidents occur per million takeoff attempts, helping explain why certain types of aircraft are more common or have longer histories. The A310 was introduced in 1983 and has the highest rate of complete destruction among all current passenger aircraft (2.53 per 1 million takeoffs), while the A320 series (including A321 and A319neo) has the lowest rate at 0.11. The Boeing 737 Max series began service in 2017 with a rate of 1.48.
It’s worth remembering that even though these rates are calculated per million takeoffs, some of the aircraft types reported here (such as the Boeing 727 introduced in 1964) have been in service for decades, so there will naturally be longer periods of time to accumulate accident records compared to other types.
Media representative Perry Kupper of Seattle-Tacoma International Airport’s media office expressed in an email that causes of accidents range from equipment failures to human error. While it’s possible to compare flight types based on available data, the FAA doesn’t recommend trying to do so by comparing airline accident records. An administrative department’s report points out that “there is no evidence currently available to support ranking airlines by their safety record.
While airline safety records can vary significantly at any given point in time, the FAA notes that much of this variation is due to “the frequency and severity of air crashes,” and that these significant differences tend to level out over time. The FAA declined to comment.
